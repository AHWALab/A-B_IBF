IBF for Antigua and Barbuda: Method and Design Note

1. Purpose

This note documents the impact-based forecasting (IBF) product delivered for Antigua and Barbuda with the Tropical Storm Jerry hindcast of 9 to 11 October 2025. The receptor routine (tito_utils.ibf_utils) runs inside the TITO orchestrator immediately after the flood inundation mapping step of each parish site and converts the flood likelihood products of the site into warning levels for every building, road segment and parish. The viewer beside this note shows those levels for each of the 48 hourly forecast cycles.

2. Inputs

Likelihood. For each parish (Barbuda and the six parishes of Antigua) a library of 200 pre-simulated flood maps from the hydrodynamic island models, windowed on the parish and indexed on the RainyDay storm total over the parish. In each cycle every one of the 50 ensemble members (10 STREAM-Sat analysis members by 5 StormLab rainfall realizations, 24 h of forecast rain) is matched to the library by its rainfall total over the parish, and the members vote pixel by pixel. The products used here are the probabilities of exceeding 0.10, 0.30, 0.70 and 1.00 m, on the 30 m grid of the TITO domain.

Receptors. Overture Maps buildings (71,136 in the seven units) and roads (9,559 segments, 1,237 km, classes motorway to unclassified) shipped in the TITO repository, classified with the GHS BUILT-C functional layer (residential, non-residential, other), joined to the ADM1 parishes with the Statistics Division population estimates, and given a dasymetric population per building by area weight and functional class. Critical facilities are the Overture subtypes medical, education, civic and emergency where present (78 buildings carry the flag).

3. The adopted standard

The product follows Speight et al. (2018), the Glasgow operational surface water IBF pilot, whose framework is the Scottish Flood Forecasting Service and UK Flood Guidance Statement standard. The warning level of every feature is the matrix combination of likelihood and potential impact severity, rendered green, yellow, amber and red.

Likelihood band (FGS)Probability of occurrence
Very Low5 to 20 percent
Low20 to 40 percent
Medium40 to 60 percent
Highabove 60 percent

Probabilities below 5 percent fall under the reporting threshold. Each severity level is tied to a depth threshold of the library products: Minor to 0.10 m, Significant to 0.30 m, Severe to 0.70 m, the same values for every country of the project since version 1.3.1. The probability of each threshold is sampled on every receptor as the maximum over its footprint, gives that severity's likelihood band, and the feature colour is the worst matrix cell across the three severities. This keeps likelihood and severity as two axes instead of collapsing them through one probability cutoff, so a low probability of severe flooding still yields an amber cell. No velocity field exists for the island libraries, so the flood hazard rating upgrade of the Barbados scenario product is not applied.

For continuity with the IBF team's IBFv1.0 fields, the routine also writes the hazard flag (highest threshold whose probability reaches the cutoff of 50 percent, the user default; the reference runs used 30 percent), the residential and hazard class breakdowns per parish, the impact warning flags on four exposure metrics (population, building count, building area, road length: severe at 10 affected or 5 percent, otherwise low, moderate and high bins at 100 or 10 percent of the parish stock) and the parish impact flag, which the matrix combines with the parish likelihood into the parish overall risk shown in the viewer.

4. Assembly for the viewer

Each parish library is windowed on the bounding box of the parish, so the receptor windows of neighbouring parishes overlap and a building near a boundary is classified by two sites with two different rainfall totals. The viewer keeps, for every receptor, the classification of the parish that contains it, so the country totals count each building and road once. Parish sites that stay quiet in a cycle (no run over the trigger of 1.0 m3/s per km2 over the parish window) produce no product and are shown as quiet. The 12:00 UTC cycle of 9 October did not complete and is absent.

5. What the hindcast shows

The warning builds in three steps. The cycles of 9 October before 12:00 UTC carry a few hundred yellow buildings at most. From 13:00 UTC on 9 October the count rises to about 2,200 yellow and 80 amber buildings and holds through the evening. The cycles issued from 00:00 UTC on 10 October, five hours before the observed rain burst at V.C. Bird International Airport (167 mm in four hours from 04:40 UTC), reach 2,700 to 2,900 yellow, 200 to 250 amber and 15 to 20 red buildings, 3,700 people in buildings at yellow or worse, and five of the seven parishes at HIGH overall risk. The levels hold through the burst and recede from 18:00 UTC on 10 October. The reported impacts of the event (Piggotts, the Airport Road intersection, Big Creek, Bathlodge, Friars Hill Road, Folleys, the Meteorological Office at the airport, and rising water in St. John's, Bolans, All Saints and Clare Hall) are drawn on the map for comparison.

6. Named facilities, crossings and roads from OpenStreetMap

The training package holds an OpenStreetMap extract of Antigua and Barbuda dated 6 April 2026 (40,048 building footprints with their tags, 9,113 road lines, land use polygons). It is less complete than the Overture receptor base as a building inventory (71,136 footprints), so it does not replace the receptors; it adds what the receptor base lacks: names and functions. Three things were derived from it for the viewer.

Facilities. Tagged buildings were grouped into nine classes: health (hospitals, clinics, dentists, pharmacies), education (schools, colleges, universities, kindergartens), emergency services (police, fire), shelters and community centres, places of worship (the usual hurricane shelters of the islands), government offices, utilities and transport (power, storage tanks, airport terminals, the APUA headquarters), fuel stations, banks and supermarkets, and hotels or guest houses: 275 facilities in all, 188 of them named. Each facility is classified per cycle with the rules of the receptor routine, sampling the parish likelihood mosaics over its footprint; on the building receptors of the peak cycle this sampling reproduces the routine's warning class for 99.5 percent of the 3,174 receptors compared, the rest being boundary cells where the routine read a neighbouring parish window.

Crossings. Road lines tagged as bridges (23) or fords (2) form a crossings receptor class, classified the same way over a 10 m buffer. They are the receptors that respond first and strongest to the flash flood signal: 16 of the 25 reach yellow or worse in the cycles issued from 00:00 UTC on 10 October, with the Sir Sidney Walling Highway bridge and the Valley Road bridge at amber and one secondary road bridge at red, hours before the observed rain burst.

Names. Road receptors that ever reach yellow were matched to the named OpenStreetMap lines (largest overlap within 15 m): 506 of the 879 segments carry a name, which the viewer uses in the popups and in a per cycle list of named roads at amber or worse (Fig Tree Drive, Old Parham Road, Collins Road, Golf Club Road, Freemans Road and Burma Road among the roads that reach red). Building receptors that fall inside a tagged OpenStreetMap footprint show its name or function in their popup. The OpenStreetMap land use polygons (residential, commercial, industrial, military) are available as a context layer.

Facilities stay mostly at yellow in this event: 14 reach yellow at some point (a clinic, two places of worship, a police station, a supermarket, schools and university buildings in Saint George, two guest houses, two storage tanks), none reach amber. Bridges, fords and the named roads carry the amber and red levels, which matches the reported impacts (flooded roads and intersections).

7. Open items for the next iteration

  1. Parish roll up thresholds: the IBFv1.0 impact flags reach severe with 10 affected buildings, which sends most parishes to HIGH once any cluster of red buildings appears. The thresholds are configuration values and should be set jointly with the national partners.
  2. Library windows: clipping the parish libraries to the parish polygons, or masking the products, would remove the double classification near boundaries and the need for the own parish rule in the viewer.
  3. Multi site output folders: in the orchestrated run every parish wrote its likelihood products to the same folder, so only the last site's rasters survived in the archive. The receptor products were correct because each ran right after its own site; the mosaics of the training package were rebuilt from the archived members. A site subfolder in the FIM output path fixes this for the operational chain.
  4. Coastal and drainage mechanisms: the libraries are pluvial; storm surge, tidal locking and drainage failure are not represented.
  5. Population: parish estimates disaggregated to buildings are an indicator; an enumeration district layer, as used for Barbados, would sharpen the exposure figures.
  6. Facility inventory: 87 of the 275 OpenStreetMap facilities are unnamed and many schools, clinics and shelters are missing from the map data; a national list of critical facilities and official shelters, with coordinates, would complete this receptor class.
